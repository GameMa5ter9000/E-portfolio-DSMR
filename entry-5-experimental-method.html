The Logic of a True Experiment — and Why This Study Can't Quite Be One

This entry looks at experimental research: what makes a study a true experiment, whether that design fits a classroom intervention like this one, and what a fully experimental version of the project would look like if it were possible.

01 · experimental methods: useful or not?

Partially Useful — the Logic Fits, the Randomization Doesn't

Logic borrowed, not the full design

A true experiment requires randomly assigning individual participants to conditions, which is simply not possible here: the school provides two pre-existing, intact groups of third graders, and reshuffling children across sections is not something a researcher can do in a real classroom. So this study cannot be a true experiment in the strict sense.

Even so, the core experimental logic is exactly what this proposal relies on: deliberately manipulating an independent variable — the teaching method, matching game versus workbook practice — and measuring its effect on a dependent variable, vocabulary test scores, while trying to keep every other condition (time, teacher, words taught) the same for both groups. That shared logic is what carries over into the quasi-experimental design explored in the next entry.

02 · project example — the ideal, fully experimental version

If a True Experiment Were Possible

hypothetical design, for comparison only — not the design actually used
1

Draw a pool of third-grade volunteers from several sections and randomly assign each student to either the matching-game condition or the workbook condition.

2

Apply the same vocabulary pretest to both randomly formed groups to confirm they start out equivalent.

3

Deliver the treatment: the matching game for one group, workbook practice for the other, under identical time and word conditions.

4

Apply an immediate posttest, then a delayed posttest two weeks later, to both groups.

⚠ not used in this study — the school's classroom groups are already formed and cannot be randomized
03 · guiding questions

Understanding Experiments

What is an experiment, and what are its main components?

An experiment is a research method in which the researcher deliberately manipulates one or more independent variables under controlled conditions in order to observe their effect on a dependent variable. Its main components are manipulation of the independent variable, control of outside factors, comparison between conditions, and — in a true experiment — random assignment of participants.

What different settings do researchers carry out experiments in?

Experiments can take place in a laboratory setting, where conditions are tightly controlled and artificial, or in a field setting, such as a real classroom, where conditions are more natural but harder to control. This study, if it were experimental, would be a field experiment.

What are the different ways a researcher might manipulate an independent variable?

A researcher can manipulate presence versus absence (the game is used or it isn't), amount or intensity (more or fewer minutes of game practice), or type (one kind of game activity versus another, such as matching cards versus bingo). This study manipulates type: matching-game practice versus workbook practice.

What is experimental control, and how does it relate to differential influence?

Experimental control is the researcher's effort to hold every condition constant except the independent variable being tested — same time, same words, same teacher — usually achieved through randomization and standardized procedures. Differential influence happens when something other than the independent variable differs systematically between groups; strong experimental control is what allows a researcher to say any difference in outcomes was caused by the treatment and not by some other, uncontrolled difference between the groups.
