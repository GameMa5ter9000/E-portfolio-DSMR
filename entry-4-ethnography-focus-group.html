Ethnographic Studies and a Focus Group Instrument

This entry weighs ethnography against the needs of the matching-game study, revisits the field notes method chosen in Entry #3, and introduces a short focus group designed to hear the students' own voices about the game.

01 · ethnographic studies: useful or not?

Why Full Ethnography Is Not the Core Design — But a Small Piece Fits

Not the main design

Classic ethnography asks a researcher to spend an extended time immersed in a group — often months — to build a thick, detailed description of a whole classroom culture: its routines, relationships, and unwritten rules. This study runs over a few weeks and asks a narrower, comparative question (does the matching game beat workbook practice on vocabulary scores), so a full ethnographic design would be a poor match for its scope and timeline.

Even so, one ethnographic-style tool still earns a place here: the focus group. It is a light, one-time way to hear students describe the activity in their own words — something neither the vocabulary test nor the teacher's field notes can capture directly. Used this way, it does not turn the study into an ethnography; it simply borrows one of ethnography's tools to enrich the objective on student perceptions of the game.

02 · possible sample

Who Would Take Part in the Focus Group

Rather than involving the whole class, the focus group would be kept small on purpose — around five to six volunteer students from Group A (the matching-game group). Small groups work better with children this age: everyone gets a turn to speak, the conversation stays manageable for one adult to guide, and shy students are less likely to be talked over than in a full-class discussion.

Who

5–6 students from Group A, chosen voluntarily, with parental/guardian consent already on file.

When

Once, right after the last matching-game session — while the experience is still fresh.

03 · revisiting the chosen method

Field Notes, and Why a Focus Group Adds to Them

The data collection method designed in the previous session was classroom observation through field notes, chosen because vocabulary test scores show whether learning happened but not what the activity actually looked like while students were doing it. Field notes let the teacher-researcher record attention, participation, and small moments of struggle or success, session by session, building a running account that supports the test results and feeds the objective on teacher and student perceptions.

What field notes cannot do is capture the students' own account in their own words — that observation is always filtered through the adult observer. The focus group closes that gap: a short, guided conversation where the students themselves describe what they liked, found hard, or remembered, adding a participant-voiced layer to the teacher's observational one.

04 · designed instrument

Focus Group Guide

one session · Group A volunteers · led by the teacher-researcher
Group size5–6 students
Duration~15 minutes
SettingFamiliar classroom corner
FormatOpen, guided talk
Warm-up Show me with your hand — did you like playing with the picture cards? 👍 👎
Question 1 What was your favorite part of the matching game?
Question 2 Was there anything that felt hard or confusing while you were playing?
Question 3 Can you remember one of the new words? Can you show me the picture that goes with it?
Question 4 Would you like to play this game again with other words, like animals or food?
Ethical note Because participants are young children, the focus group would run only with prior parental/guardian consent and the children's own simple assent, use plain and friendly language, and record only written notes of what is said rather than audio or video of the children themselves — in line with the informed-consent and anti-plagiarism principles covered earlier in this course.
