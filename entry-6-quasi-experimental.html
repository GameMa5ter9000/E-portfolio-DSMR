A Nonequivalent Comparison Group Design — This Study's Real Model

Where the true experiment fell short, the quasi-experimental, nonequivalent comparison group design fits perfectly. This entry lays out the actual design this study will use, and works through the guiding questions on causal inference and design features.

01 · quasi-experimental design: useful or not?

Yes — This Is the Design the Study Has Been Building Toward

Core design

A nonequivalent comparison group design compares two or more intact, pre-existing groups instead of randomly assigned ones — which is exactly the situation in this study: one class, already split into its own natural groups, with no way to reshuffle students. Adding a pretest to check how similar the groups are before the intervention gives the comparison real strength, even without randomization.

This confirms what Entries #1 and #2 already assumed: the matching-game group and the workbook group are compared as they naturally exist, not as randomly formed conditions — which makes this, formally, a quasi-experimental, nonequivalent comparison group design.

03 · guiding questions

Understanding Quasi-Experimental Designs

What are the requirements for a valid causal inference in a quasi-experimental design?

Without random assignment, a valid causal inference depends on a pretest that shows the groups started out reasonably equivalent, a comparison group that experiences the same timing and testing conditions, and careful attention to threats like history, maturation, or selection bias that could explain a difference in outcomes besides the treatment itself.

What is a nonequivalent comparison group design, and what are its main characteristics?

It is a design that compares two or more intact, pre-existing groups rather than randomly assigned ones. Its main characteristics are the lack of random assignment, the use of a pretest to check for initial group differences, and reliance on statistical or design-based reasoning — rather than randomization — to argue that the treatment, not some other factor, explains the results.

What are the main design features of an interrupted time-series design?

An interrupted time-series design follows a single group with repeated measurements taken at multiple points before and after an intervention, rather than comparing it to a separate group. The "interruption" is the treatment itself, and the design looks for a visible change in the trend line right after that point — it needs several data points on each side to tell a real change from normal variation.
