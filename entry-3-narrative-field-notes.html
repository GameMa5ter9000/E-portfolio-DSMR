Narrative Studies, the Research Sample, and a Field Notes Instrument

This entry weighs narrative inquiry against the needs of the matching-game study, sketches who the participants will be, and introduces the observation instrument chosen to complement the vocabulary test scores.

← Entry #2: Data collection methods  ·  Front page

01 · narrative studies: useful or not?

Why Narrative Studies Are Not the Core Design — But Still Have a Place

Not the main design

Narrative inquiry centers on individual life stories, told over time and interpreted for meaning — it is not built to compare test scores between two groups. Since this study's core question asks whether the matching-game group learns and retains more vocabulary than the workbook group, a quasi-experimental, nonequivalent-groups design remains the right fit for the main data (immediate and delayed vocabulary tests).

Even so, narrative elements are not entirely absent from this proposal. One of the specific objectives is to describe how the teacher and students perceive the practicality and ease of use of the matching game in this classroom. That is a small, situated story about one class's experience — exactly the kind of thing narrative-adjacent tools like teacher journals and short student comments are good at capturing. So narrative studies are set aside as the overall research design, but a light narrative thread survives as a way to enrich the quantitative results with the teacher's own account of what happened in the room.

02 · possible sample

Who the Study Would Involve

The sample would be drawn from a single third-grade class (around eight and nine years old) at a Colombian public primary school, split into two intact, naturally-occurring groups rather than randomly assigned individuals — which is typical of classroom-based, nonequivalent-groups research.

Group A — Matching game

Practises classroom-object vocabulary through the picture–word matching game during the intervention sessions.

Group B — Workbook practice

Practises the same vocabulary through the regular workbook-based activities the teacher already uses.

03 · chosen data collection method

Classroom Observation: Field Notes

Why field notes

Vocabulary test scores can show whether learning happened, but not what it looked like while it happened. Field notes let the researcher record, in the moment, how each group engages with its activity — attention, participation, and the small moments of struggle or success that a test score alone cannot show.

How it will be used

The teacher-researcher will complete one field notes sheet per session for each group, taking brief descriptive notes during the activity and adding a short reflective note afterward. Over the weeks of the intervention, these notes build a running, situated account that supports and helps explain the quantitative test results — and feeds directly into the objective on teacher and student perceptions of the game.
